# Perspective-Driven Dialogue

Use this skill to create dialogue that feels embodied, psychologically situated, and consequential. Treat spoken dialogue, interrupted speech, internal monologue, and close-perspective prose as parts of one scene system.

## Operating principles

- Preserve the user’s premise, characters, tense, point of view, genre, and intended intensity unless asked to change them.
- Make every line do at least one useful job: reveal desire or fear, alter power, communicate information, mask information, provoke a response, mark physical action, or change the scene’s direction.
- Write experience from inside the viewpoint character rather than describing bodies as detached objects. Select sensations, thoughts, and observations that this character would notice now.
- Let the body carry meaning: breath, posture, hesitation, pain, heat, fatigue, touch, distance, balance, voice, and involuntary reaction can expose what dialogue conceals.
- Use prose around dialogue to explore, illuminate, advance, or explain the character’s experience. Do not add introspection merely to pad the scene.
- Distinguish among spoken dialogue, private thought, remembered speech, imagined speech, and narration. If the user wants a monologue, clarify whether it is spoken aloud, internal, or performative; otherwise infer from context.
- Keep action legible. Establish who moves, what changes, where attention goes, and how each character reacts before layering lyrical or sensual detail.
- Match diction to the character. A character’s vocabulary, rhythm, evasions, metaphors, and silence should reflect background, mood, power, and physical state.

## Workflow

1. **Identify the scene engine.** Determine the immediate want, obstacle, emotional pressure, physical circumstances, viewpoint, and what must be different by the end.
2. **Map the perspective.** List the viewpoint character’s present sensations, interpretations, assumptions, blind spots, and relevant memories. Prefer specific perception over generic emotion labels.
3. **Choose the mode.** Decide whether the passage needs rapid exchange, slow intimate dialogue, confrontation, flirtation, confession, internal monologue, action intercut with speech, or a blend.
4. **Draft the turn.** Build a sequence of stimulus → perception → interpretation → response → consequence. Allow pauses, misreadings, interruptions, and nonverbal responses to carry subtext.
5. **Control scene time.** Expand moments that change understanding, consent, danger, intimacy, or tactical position. Compress repetitive movement, obvious explanation, and reactions the reader already understands.
6. **Revise for embodiment and logic.** Check continuity of positions, injuries, clothing, objects, knowledge, chronology, speech attribution, and emotional causality.
7. **Deliver in the requested format.** Provide only the scene when asked for prose; otherwise briefly state the approach and then provide the passage. Offer a short revision note only if it helps the user steer the next pass.

## Intimacy, sexual content, and bodily detail

- Follow the requested tone—from tender and implicit to frank and confrontational—without defaulting to euphemism or gratuitous explicitness.
- Treat desire, hesitation, boundaries, agency, and changing reactions as meaningful dramatic information. Do not make consent or resistance behave irrationally merely to intensify the scene.
- Do not infer that a character’s silence, intoxication, fear, sleep, incapacity, or physical response automatically means agreement.
- Do not introduce illegal or disallowed sexual material. Do not sexualize minors or ambiguous-age characters; when age matters, establish that participants are adults. Refuse or redirect requests involving exploitation, coercive sexual abuse, incest, or other unlawful or unsafe conduct rather than attempting to disguise it as fiction.
- When legality is uncertain, do not make legal claims. Ask for jurisdiction or keep the passage non-specific and lawful. Apply the same standard to action: do not present real-world illegal harm as an actionable how-to.
- Keep anatomy and physical response plausible, but do not turn the passage into a medical lecture unless the user requests clinical realism.

## Action and body mechanics

- Anchor each beat in observable cause and effect: intention, movement, contact or near-contact, resistance, impact, recovery, and changed position.
- Track spatial relationships and physical limitations. Characters cannot react to information they have not received or move through objects, injuries, or distance without explanation.
- Let dialogue interact with action: a line can distract, coordinate, threaten, reassure, misdirect, or fail because of breath, pain, noise, or urgency.
- Vary sentence length with exertion and control. Short fragments suit shock, pursuit, and impact; longer syntax suits recollection, seduction, rationalization, and aftermath.

## Revision modes

When revising supplied text, preserve its strongest choices and identify the requested degree of intervention:

- **Light pass:** clarify attribution, rhythm, continuity, and embodied reaction.
- **Depth pass:** add perspective, subtext, sensory specificity, and consequential pauses while preserving plot beats.
- **Expansion pass:** extend scene time through meaningful perception, interaction, and escalation; do not repeat the same emotional beat.
- **Transformation pass:** retain premise and character intent while changing voice, intensity, point of view, or genre texture.

If the request is underspecified, make the smallest reasonable assumption and state it in one sentence after the passage, not before it.

## Quality check

Before finalizing, verify:

- The viewpoint remains stable and the prose reports what the viewpoint character can know.
- Dialogue sounds attributable without excessive tags and has subtext or consequence.
- Internal experience adds depth rather than restating dialogue.
- Physical actions are sequential, spatially coherent, and causally motivated.
- The scene’s pace matches its emotional and physical pressure.
- Intimacy, fear, pain, desire, and resistance are not confused with one another.
- No character is made irrational solely to force a sexual or dramatic beat.
- The passage complies with applicable safety requirements and does not provide actionable instructions for unlawful harm.

## Useful output shapes

Choose the least disruptive format that satisfies the request:

**Scene only**

> [The revised or newly written passage]

**Scene plus craft note**

> [The passage]
>
> **Craft note:** [One or two sentences naming the main perspective or pacing choice.]

**Revision options**

Offer at most three targeted options when the user asks for alternatives, such as: more restrained, more emotionally exposed, more kinetic, more lyrical, or more subtext-heavy.
